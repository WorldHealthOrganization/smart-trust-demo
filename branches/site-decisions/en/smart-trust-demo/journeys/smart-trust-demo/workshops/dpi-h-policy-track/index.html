    Verifiable immunization records across borders: the policy-maker track

    The seven modules (M1 to M7) of the DPI-H workshop's policy-maker track, taught from the journeys J1 to J4.1 of one cross-border family: domestic and cross-border verifiable immunization records as the entry point to a personal health record.

    For: Ministry of Health policy makers and digital health leads, at tables of one country each

    You will not leave with an assessment or a plan. You will leave knowing what each consists of, where your country stands on its first page, and who you would do the rest with.

    A child vaccinated in ___ should be able to ___ in ___.

    How a table rates what it needs

    • Where we stand: Exists · Partial · Absent
    • How urgent: Blocks us now · Needed within 12 months · Can wait

    “Absent” is a finding, not a failure — the question is only whether it blocks this use case.

    Closing discussion

    1. M2 Identity for babies born abroad J1 J2b

      How should national immunization systems handle temporary IDs for cross-border infants before civil registration is complete?

    2. M4 Where trust begins J2a J3a

      Why should offline CHW devices store foreign cards as “reported, not verified” rather than trying to verify them in the field?

    3. M3 Matching vaccines across countries J3b

      When a monovalent vaccine is given abroad but the home schedule uses a combination (e.g. pentavalent), how should schedule rules calculate the doses still due?

    4. M5 Cross-border access and audit J4.0b J4.1

      What agreements do countries need so that a clinician viewing a record abroad triggers an audit entry back in the patient’s home country?

    • reading Terms at J1
      • Electronic immunization registry (EIR): Records each person's doses. Often the first working part of a lifelong record.
      • Client Registry: The Client Registry is the national authoritative source of health-specific person identity. It is the foundational health-sector component that maintains a unique, verifiable identity record for every person who accesses or is eligible to access health services within a jurisdiction.
      • Facility, workforce and product registries: Authoritative lists of health facilities, health workers, and products such as vaccines and their batches.
      • Dose number and series: Which dose this is, out of how many in the schedule. Needed to work out what is due next.
      • Batch number: Identifies the production batch of a vaccine. Needed for recalls and safety checks.
      • Verifiable credential (VC): A digitally signed statement, such as a vaccination record, that anyone can check is genuine and unaltered.
      • Digital signature: A cryptographic seal made with the issuer's private key. Verifiers check it with the matching public key.
      • Public key infrastructure (PKI): The keys, certificates and rules that make digital signatures trustworthy.
      • Issuer · holder · verifier: The three roles: who signs the credential, who keeps it, and who checks it.
      • Health certificate identifier (HCID): The unique ID of an issued certificate.
      • International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis (ICVP): The "yellow card". The digital ICVP is its signed, verifiable form.
      • Consent: A person's permission for their data to be used or shared. Rules for minors and dependants differ by country.
    • reading Terms at J2a
      • Point-of-Service Applications: Point-of-service applications are end-user application components (IT systems or software tools) used by individuals directly engaged in healthcare—such as clinicians, community health workers, and patients—that enable interaction with the broader healthcare ecosystem.
      • Verifiable credential (VC): A digitally signed statement, such as a vaccination record, that anyone can check is genuine and unaltered.
      • Digital signature: A cryptographic seal made with the issuer's private key. Verifiers check it with the matching public key.
      • Core data set: The elements a country agrees to record for a use case, each mapped to standard codes. Also called a minimum data set.

It is the foundational health-sector component that maintains a unique, verifiable identity record for every person who accesses or is eligible to access health services within a jurisdiction.

    • reading Terms at J3a
      • Trust list: A published list of keys accepted as valid issuers. The GDHCN shares these between countries.
      • Global Digital Health Certification Network (GDHCN): WHO Global Digital Health Certification Network. A trust network so countries can verify each other's health documents. Countries onboard per use case.
      • Trust anchor: The national authority whose key vouches for the health sector's signers.

    • reading Terms at J3b
      • Terminology Service: The Terminology Service is a governed, publicly accessible service that manages, publishes, and provides access to the standardised terminologies, code systems, value sets, and concept maps used across the digital health ecosystem.
      • Core data set: The elements a country agrees to record for a use case, each mapped to standard codes. Also called a minimum data set.
      • Logical information model: The agreed structure of the data: elements, types and rules, independent of any software.
      • SNOMED CT: Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms is a structured clinical terminology standard for use in electronic health records.
      • Computable Decision Support Engine: The Computable Decision Support Engine is a governed, shared application component that delivers context-appropriate, evidence-based guidance at the point of clinical decision-making by applying computable clinical rules and knowledge artefacts to person-specific health data.
      • SMART Guidelines: Standards-based, Machine-readable, Adaptive, Requirements-based, Testable Guidelines are a comprehensive set of reusable digital health components (e.g., interoperability standards, code libraries, algorithms, technical and operational specifications) that transform the guideline adaptation and implementation process to preserve fidelity and accelerate uptake. SMART Guidelines provide a five-step pathway to advance the adoption of best clinical and data practices, even if a country is not yet fully digital.
      • Digital adaptation kit (DAK): Software-neutral L2 content: personas, workflows, core data dictionary, decision logic, indicators and requirements.
      • Dose number and series: Which dose this is, out of how many in the schedule. Needed to work out what is due next.
      • Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI): The national immunization programme.
    • reading Terms at J4.0a
      • Digital wallet: An app where a person holds their credentials. A signed paper QR code can play the same role.
      • Personal health record (PHR): Health information the person can see and control, for example in a wallet or citizen app.
      • Lifelong Health Record: The Lifelong Health Record (LHR) is one or more governed data repositories, whether physically consolidated or virtually federated, that collectively maintain a longitudinal, person-centred record of an individual's key health information across care settings and over their lifetime.
      • Consent: A person's permission for their data to be used or shared. Rules for minors and dependants differ by country.

      • Profile: A FHIR resource tailored to a use case, for example by making some fields required.
